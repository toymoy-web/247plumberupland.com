When a home's interior supply lines are failing in multiple places, repiping the whole house is often more cost-effective than chasing one leak after another.

Repiping replaces a home's interior supply lines, not the sewer or the incoming service line from the street. It's the right call when galvanized or polybutylene pipe throughout the house is aging out and producing repeated leaks in different spots rather than one isolated failure.

This is a different job from a single leak repair or the water line from the meter to the house. If you're not sure which situation you're in, we can assess during a leak detection visit before recommending a full repipe.

The Cause, Explained

Why Older Upland Homes Need Repiping

Galvanized steel pipe, common in homes built before the 1960s, corrodes from the inside over decades until the interior diameter narrows and pressure drops, eventually leading to pinhole leaks in multiple spots.

Polybutylene pipe, used in some homes built between the late 1970s and mid-1990s, is prone to breaking down from the inside due to chlorine exposure in municipal water, often without external warning signs until a fitting fails.

Upland's hard water accelerates internal corrosion in galvanized pipe specifically, which is part of why repiping conversations come up earlier here than in a soft-water market.

Signs To Watch For

Signs You Need A Full Repipe

Leaks In Multiple Locations

Different spots failing over months or years points to system-wide pipe age, not a single bad section.

Persistently Low Water Pressure

Often internal corrosion narrowing the pipe diameter throughout the house.

Discolored Water From Multiple Fixtures

A sign of widespread galvanized pipe corrosion, not a single fixture issue.

Home Has Original Polybutylene Pipe

Worth a proactive assessment even without an active leak, given this material's known failure pattern.

Material Options

Copper Vs PEX

Copper

  • Longer proven track record
  • Higher material cost
  • Rigid, more joints needed
  • Naturally resistant to bacteria

PEX

  • Lower material and labor cost
  • Flexible, fewer fittings needed
  • Faster installation timeline
  • Slightly shorter proven lifespan than copper
Minimizing Disruption

How We Limit Wall And Ceiling Access

A full repipe doesn't have to mean opening every wall in the house. We plan routing to minimize access points, often running new lines through attics, crawlspaces, or existing chases where possible, and patch any drywall access points as part of the project.

FAQs

Common Questions

How long does a whole-home repipe take?

Most residential repipes take two to five days depending on home size and material choice.

Will I have water during the project?

We coordinate scheduling to minimize downtime, though water is typically off during active work each day.

Copper or PEX, which is better?

Both are code-compliant and reliable. Copper has a longer track record. PEX is faster to install and costs less. We can walk through the tradeoffs for your specific home.

Do I need to repipe if I only have one leak?

Not necessarily. A single leak in an otherwise sound system is usually a standard repair, not a full repipe.

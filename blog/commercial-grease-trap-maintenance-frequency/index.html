Commercial Grease Trap Maintenance: How Often Is Enough?

There's no single right answer for every kitchen, but there is a reliable, practical way to figure out the right schedule for yours before a backup forces the question.

Restaurant owners and property managers often ask us for a flat answer, monthly, quarterly, how often should a grease trap actually be serviced. The honest answer is that it depends on kitchen volume, menu type, and trap size, but there's a straightforward, repeatable way to figure out the right interval for a specific property rather than guessing or copying a neighbor's schedule.

Why A Generic Schedule Doesn't Work

A high-volume fryer-heavy kitchen produces grease at a completely different rate than a coffee shop or a light-prep café. Applying the same maintenance interval to both means one property is either paying for unnecessary service, or worse, running past the point where a backup becomes a real risk during business hours.

Trap size matters too. A larger trap has more capacity before it needs pumping, which can extend the interval even for a similarly busy kitchen, while an undersized trap for the kitchen's actual volume may need attention more often regardless of how careful staff are about what goes down the drain.

Staff training also plays a real role. Two kitchens with identical equipment and menus can have very different grease trap timelines depending on how consistently staff scrape plates and pans before washing, versus letting grease and food waste go straight down the drain.

The 25 Percent Rule

A widely used industry guideline is the 25 percent rule: a grease trap should be pumped once accumulated grease and solids reach about 25 percent of the trap's total capacity. Beyond that point, the trap's effective capacity shrinks enough that it starts passing grease downstream instead of capturing it, which is exactly what leads to line backups.

The only reliable way to apply this rule to your specific kitchen is to have the trap actually inspected on a regular basis until a clear pattern emerges, rather than guessing at an interval from the start.

How To Actually Set Your Schedule

Start with more frequent inspections, monthly is reasonable for a new or unknown kitchen, and track how quickly the trap reaches that 25 percent threshold each time. Once you've observed the pattern over two or three inspection cycles, you can set a maintenance interval that matches your kitchen's real grease production instead of a generic industry average.

Seasonal menu changes, catering volume spikes, or an increase in fried menu items can all shift that interval over time, which is why it's worth revisiting the schedule periodically rather than setting it once and assuming it stays accurate indefinitely.

Keeping a simple log of each inspection's findings, not just the pump-out dates, makes this pattern tracking much easier. Noting the percentage full at each check gives you actual data to base schedule adjustments on rather than relying on memory or guesswork months later.

What Happens If You Skip Maintenance

A backup during service hours is the worst-case scenario for any commercial kitchen, shutting down operations at the least convenient possible moment. Beyond the immediate disruption, many jurisdictions also have compliance requirements around grease trap maintenance records, and a documented maintenance history matters if a backup does happen and needs to be traced back to a cause.

Regular hydro jetting alongside scheduled pumping also helps keep the connected drain lines themselves clear, since grease that makes it past the trap can still build up further down the line over time even with a well-maintained trap.

There's also a cost dimension beyond the disruption itself. An emergency call to clear a fully backed-up line during business hours typically costs more than routine scheduled maintenance would have, on top of the lost revenue from any service interruption.

What A Typical Service Visit Involves

A standard grease trap pump-out involves removing accumulated grease, solids, and wastewater from the trap, then inspecting the trap's internal baffles and components for damage or wear. Any documentation required for local compliance is typically provided at this time as well.

For properties on a hydro jetting schedule alongside trap pumping, the two services are often coordinated on the same visit, since a technician already on site for one can efficiently address the other without a separate scheduling and travel cost.

We also flag any early signs of a failing baffle or damaged component during the visit, since catching a mechanical issue with the trap itself early is far less disruptive than discovering it only after it's already contributed to a backup.

What to check when setting your maintenance schedule

  • Start with monthly inspections if you don't have historical data on your trap
  • Track how quickly the trap reaches roughly 25 percent capacity each cycle
  • Factor in seasonal menu changes or catering volume spikes
  • Keep documented maintenance records for compliance purposes
  • Coordinate jetting and pumping visits together when both are due around the same time

FAQs

Is the 25 percent rule a legal requirement or just a guideline?

It's an industry guideline, though some jurisdictions incorporate similar thresholds into local grease trap compliance requirements. We can help clarify what applies to your specific property.

Can I service the trap myself instead of hiring it out?

Some basic monitoring can be done in-house, but proper pumping and disposal typically requires licensed service both for compliance and to avoid damaging the trap during cleaning.

Does hydro jetting replace the need for grease trap pumping?

No, they address different parts of the system. Pumping empties the trap itself. Jetting clears the connected drain lines. Most commercial kitchens benefit from both on their own schedules.

How quickly can you respond if we have an active backup?

We prioritize commercial calls that affect business operations and can typically dispatch the same day for an active kitchen backup.

Does the type of cuisine affect how often the trap fills?

Yes, heavily fried or fatty menus fill a trap faster than a lighter menu, which is part of why the 25 percent rule needs to be checked against your own kitchen rather than a generic industry number.

Can a poorly maintained grease trap affect the whole building's plumbing?

In multi-tenant commercial buildings, yes. Grease that bypasses an overdue trap can affect shared drain lines serving other tenants, which is one more reason to keep to a schedule rather than push maintenance off.

Renovating An Older Upland Home? Check The Plumbing First

Before new tile, flooring, or cabinetry goes in, it's worth confirming the plumbing behind the walls is actually in good shape. Fixing it after is far more expensive.

Renovation projects on older homes, common across downtown Upland's Historic District and other established neighborhoods, tend to focus on visible finishes first: tile, fixtures, cabinetry, paint. The plumbing behind the walls often only gets attention if something actively fails during the project, which is exactly the wrong time to discover a problem.

A plumbing assessment before finishes go in costs far less than tearing out new tile or flooring to fix something that should have been addressed first.

Why This Matters More In Older Homes Specifically

A home built before the 1960s may still have some galvanized steel supply lines, which corrode from the inside and have a predictable, well-understood failure pattern. A home from the 1970s through 90s might have polybutylene pipe in some sections, a material known for breaking down internally without external warning signs.

Neither of these situations announces itself clearly before a renovation. The pipes might be functioning adequately today and still be within a few years of a failure that would mean cutting into brand new tile or flooring to access them.

The same logic applies to a home's sewer line, especially in older, tree-lined neighborhoods where root intrusion has had decades to develop quietly. A renovation that finishes a bathroom or kitchen without checking the connected drain line risks the same outcome: new finishes disturbed months later to fix a problem that existed the whole time.

What To Actually Check Before Finishes Go In

Start with the age and material of the home's supply lines. If it's original galvanized pipe, or if you're not sure, this is worth confirming before committing to a full renovation scope, since the answer changes what work should happen in what order.

Check water pressure throughout the house, not just at one fixture, since uneven pressure can indicate a specific section of pipe that's already struggling. If a bathroom or kitchen is being fully gutted anyway, that's also the ideal moment to address any known plumbing issues in that specific area, since wall and floor access is already open.

It's also worth reviewing any permit history the home has on file with the city, if available, since prior plumbing work that wasn't permitted properly sometimes means code issues that a casual inspection won't catch but a formal renovation process will eventually surface.

The Case For A Full Repipe During A Larger Renovation

If a renovation already involves opening walls in multiple rooms, that's often the most cost-effective time to complete a full repipe, even if the existing pipe hasn't failed yet. Accessing pipe that's already exposed during a renovation is significantly cheaper than opening the same walls again later specifically for a repipe.

This isn't the right call for every renovation, a small cosmetic update to one bathroom doesn't usually justify a whole-home repipe. But for a larger project already opening multiple walls, it's worth at least having the conversation before those walls get closed back up.

Timing this correctly also avoids a frustrating scenario: finishing a beautiful renovation only to have a pipe fail somewhere in an untouched section of the house a year or two later, forcing you to cut into brand new work you just paid to complete.

Don't Forget The Sewer Line

A kitchen or bathroom renovation focuses attention on supply lines and fixtures, but it's also a reasonable time to have the home's sewer line camera-inspected, particularly in older neighborhoods with mature trees where root intrusion is common. A sewer issue discovered after a full renovation is finished is one of the more frustrating and expensive problems to deal with retroactively.

This is especially worth doing if the renovation includes relocating a toilet, sink, or any fixture that connects to the drain line, since that work already requires accessing the connection point, making a camera inspection a natural add-on rather than a separate, later disruption.

Bringing In A Plumber Before The Contractor Starts

The most effective time to have this conversation is before your general contractor's schedule locks in, not once walls are already open and the crew is mid-project. A short assessment beforehand gives everyone, you, your contractor, and your plumber, a clear picture of what's staying, what's being replaced, and in what order, before the first wall comes down.

This coordination also helps avoid schedule conflicts later, since plumbing work that turns out to be more involved than expected is far easier to absorb into a project timeline when it's identified during planning rather than discovered mid-demolition. All plumbing work follows the California Plumbing Code (UPC), regardless of when in the renovation timeline it takes place.

It also gives you real leverage in budgeting. A firm quote for known plumbing work, decided before the project starts, is far more manageable than an open-ended allowance for unknown issues that might surface once walls are already open and the project is already underway.

What to check before your renovation starts

  • Confirm your home's supply line age and material, especially for pre-1990s construction
  • Test water pressure at multiple fixtures, not just one
  • Ask whether a full repipe makes sense given how many walls are already opening
  • Consider a camera inspection of the sewer line if it hasn't been checked recently
  • Loop your plumber in before your contractor's schedule locks in, not after

FAQs

How do I know if my home has polybutylene pipe?

It's typically gray plastic pipe, common in construction from the late 1970s through mid-1990s. If you're unsure, a plumber can identify it during an assessment.

Does a plumbing assessment before renovation cost much?

It's a modest cost compared to the alternative of discovering an issue after finishes are installed and needing to tear them out to fix it.

Should I repipe even if nothing has failed yet?

If a larger renovation already has walls open in multiple rooms, it's worth considering, since the access cost is already being absorbed by the renovation itself.

Can you work around my renovation contractor's timeline?

Yes, we regularly coordinate directly with general contractors on the plumbing scope of larger renovation projects.

What if my contractor says the plumbing is fine without checking it?

General contractors focus on the visible scope of a renovation and may not assess plumbing age or condition in depth. A separate plumbing assessment is worth the modest cost for peace of mind before finishes go in.

How far in advance should I schedule a plumbing assessment before renovation?

As soon as you're seriously planning the project, ideally before finalizing a full scope and timeline with your contractor, so any plumbing findings can be factored into the schedule and budget from the very start rather than as a late surprise.

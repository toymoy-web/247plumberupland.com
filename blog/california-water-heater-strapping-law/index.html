California's Water Heater Seismic Strapping Law, Explained

Every water heater in California is legally required to be seismically strapped. Here's what that actually means, why it matters, and what to check on your own unit.

California Health and Safety Code requires all new and replacement water heaters to be braced, anchored, or strapped to resist falling during an earthquake. It's one of those requirements that's easy to overlook once a unit is installed, but it exists for a genuinely practical safety reason.

Why This Requirement Exists

An unsecured water heater can tip over during seismic activity, which creates two real hazards at once. First, the gas line feeding the unit can be damaged or sheared, creating a leak risk right at a moment when a spark or open flame nearby could ignite it. Second, the water lines can break, adding flooding to whatever else is already happening during an earthquake.

Strapping keeps the unit upright and anchored to the wall, which significantly reduces both risks. It's a genuinely low-cost safety measure relative to the hazard it prevents.

This requirement isn't unique to earthquake-prone regions in a theoretical sense, California specifically codified it because the state's seismic activity makes an unsecured water heater a documented, recurring source of gas leaks and secondary damage after moderate earthquakes, not just a hypothetical worst-case scenario.

Insurance adjusters and fire officials reviewing post-earthquake damage reports have consistently cited unsecured water heaters as a preventable contributor to gas-related fires and additional flooding, which is part of the documented basis for the code requirement rather than an arbitrary rule.

What The Code Actually Requires

The standard requirement is two straps: one in the upper third of the tank and one in the lower third, both anchored into wall studs or other solid structural framing, not just drywall. The straps need to be rated for the purpose, standard plumber's tape or improvised strapping doesn't meet code.

The exact strap placement and anchoring method can vary slightly by local jurisdiction, but the two-strap, upper-and-lower-third pattern anchored to structural framing is the consistent baseline across California.

This requirement applies regardless of whether the water heater is gas or electric, tank or tankless, and regardless of the unit's age. A brand new tankless unit still needs proper anchoring, even though its smaller size and wall-mounted design make it look less obviously at risk of tipping compared to a traditional tank.

This requirement applies regardless of the unit's fuel type or size, gas and electric water heaters alike, tank and even some tankless installations depending on mounting configuration, are all subject to the same basic seismic bracing standard.

Common Issues We Find On Existing Installations

This is one of the most frequently missed or improperly done items we see, particularly on older installations or ones done without a permit. Common problems include straps anchored into drywall rather than studs, only a single strap instead of two, straps positioned too high or too low to actually stabilize the tank effectively, or in some cases, no strapping at all on a unit that's clearly been in place for years.

Even homes where the original installation was done correctly sometimes lose compliance later, if a unit was serviced or replaced by someone who didn't restrap it properly afterward, or if the original straps have degraded or loosened over time.

We also occasionally find straps that technically exist but are positioned incorrectly relative to the tank's actual center of gravity, close enough to pass a casual glance but not actually effective at preventing the tank from tipping in a real seismic event. This is part of why a proper check matters more than just confirming straps are present at all.

Garage installations, which are extremely common throughout Upland, present their own particular challenge since garage walls often have less consistent stud spacing than interior walls, making correct anchor placement a bit more involved than a standard installation.

How To Check Your Own Unit

You can do a basic visual check yourself. Look for two straps, one near the top third and one near the bottom third of the tank, and confirm each one is anchored to something solid, typically visible as a screw or bolt going into what appears to be a wall stud location rather than just drywall.

If you're unsure whether your straps are anchored correctly or meet current code, this is something we check as a standard part of any water heater service call, and it's a quick, inexpensive fix if something needs correcting.

It's also worth checking the flexible gas and water connectors feeding the unit while you're at it. Code also generally requires flexible connectors rather than rigid piping at the water heater connection specifically, since flexible connectors absorb movement during seismic activity that rigid piping cannot, reducing the chance of a connection failure even if the strapping itself holds.

What to do before you assume your water heater is compliant

  • Confirm there are two straps, positioned in the upper and lower third of the tank
  • Check that each strap is anchored into solid framing, not just drywall
  • Look for any visible looseness, rust, or degradation in the straps themselves
  • If your unit was replaced or serviced recently, confirm strapping was redone at that time
  • Check that gas and water connections use flexible, not rigid, connectors

FAQs

Is this required for every water heater, including older ones already installed?

Yes, the requirement applies broadly in California, not just to new installations, though enforcement in practice often comes up during a sale, permit, or service visit rather than as a standalone inspection.

Can I strap my own water heater?

It's possible with the right rated strapping kit and access to solid framing, though many homeowners have it done as part of a service visit to ensure it's anchored correctly the first time.

Does this come up during a home sale?

Yes, proper water heater strapping is commonly checked during real estate inspections in California, and missing or improper strapping is a frequent, easily fixed item on inspection reports.

Will you check this even if I'm just calling for a repair, not strapping specifically?

Yes, it's something we note during any water heater visit regardless of what the original call was for, since it's a quick check with real safety value.

Does renters insurance or a landlord's policy cover this requirement?

Strapping compliance is a code requirement rather than an insurance matter, though some insurers do ask about it when underwriting or renewing a policy on a property.

Is a permit required just for adding strapping to an existing unit?

Strapping alone typically doesn't require a separate permit if the unit itself isn't being replaced, though it's worth confirming with your local jurisdiction if you're unsure about your specific situation.
